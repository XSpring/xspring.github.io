    Chapter 2: Shamir's Secret Sharing

    TL;DR. Hide the secret as the height where a random curve crosses the y-axis; any t points pin the curve and reveal it, t−1 never do.
    🎣 The hook Chapter 1 left the bank stuck: XOR sharing needs every courier, but the bank wants any 2 of 3. How do you give three slips so that any pair opens the vault and no single slip helps at all? The answer is not more masking — it is a curve.

    Prerequisites

    • Chapter 1: additive sharing, thresholds R and T, the idea that a share must look random alone
    • High-school line equation y = a₀ + a₁·x (parabola y = a₀ + a₁·x + a₂·x² helps but not required)
    • Modular arithmetic intuition: clock arithmetic with wrap-around (we compute mod a prime)

    By the end you will be able to…

    • Construct a (t,n) Shamir sharing by choosing a random polynomial through the secret
    • Reconstruct the secret from any t shares using Lagrange interpolation — by hand for t=2, 3
    • Explain in numbers why t−1 shares leave the secret uniformly random
    • Distinguish shares (points on the curve) from coefficients (the hidden shape)

    The foundational truth

    One mathematical fact powers this whole chapter: k points with distinct x determine exactly one polynomial of degree at most k−1 through them.

    One point leaves a whole family of lines through it. Two points pin a single line. Three points pin a single parabola. That jump from "infinite possibilities" to "exactly one" is the threshold.

    Shamir's trick (published alongside Blakley's hyperplane method in 1979 Shamir 1979) is to store the secret as the y-intercept — the height at x = 0 — and let the rest of the curve be random. Any k points rebuild the full curve and read off the intercept; any k−1 still leave every intercept equally possible.

    A familiar bridge — the hill and the measuring stakes

    Picture a smooth hill hidden behind a fence. You cannot see the hill, but a surveyor hands you this rule. Call it the Hill-and-Stakes Model.

    • Secret → the hill's height at the flagpole (x = 0), say 53 meters
    • Random polynomial → the hidden shape of the hill (a straight slope for 2-of-n, a parabola for 3-of-n)
    • Share → the height measured at one numbered stake: stake 1, stake 2, and so on
    • Reconstruct → hammer enough stakes to deduce the hill's shape, then read the height at the flagpole

    One stake tells you nothing about the flagpole height — many hills pass through it. Two stakes pin a straight hill. Three pin a parabola. The number of stakes that "pins" the hill is the threshold.

    ⚠️ Before we build further — most people leave this bridge thinking…

    "Each courier gets a coefficient of the polynomial — courier 1 gets the slope, courier 2 gets the curvature"

    This is wrong because coefficients directly encode the shape and the secret would need every coefficient holder — that's back to (n,n).

    What's actually true: Every courier gets a point on the curve — a height at their stake. The coefficients are never handed out and are destroyed after sharing.

    Building the model, one piece at a time

    We work over a prime modulus so division always exists. Think of it as clock arithmetic with a prime number of hours. For demos we use small primes; real systems use 256-bit primes.

    A 2-of-3 sharing with a line (degree 1)

    State

    Shamir (t,n) sharing with t = 2 hides S as the constant term of a random line f(x) = S + a₁·x mod p. Share i is the point (i, f(i)). The x-coordinate is public (stake number); the y-value is the secret slip.

    Generation: pick prime p > max(S, n), pick a₁ uniform in 0…p−1, then compute f(i) for i = 1…n.

    Illustrate

    Secret S = 53, threshold t = 2, parties n = 3, prime p = 101, random slope a₁ = 20.

    f(x) = 53 + 20·x  mod 101

f(1) = 53 + 20 = 73   → share (1, 73)
f(2) = 53 + 40 = 93   → share (2, 93)
f(3) = 53 + 60 = 113 mod 101 = 12  → share (3, 12)

    Each share looks random. Indeed, if S had been 12 with the same a₁, the shares would be (1,32),(2,52),(3,72) — no pattern betrays 53.

    Reconstruct from any 2. Suppose couriers 1 and 3 meet with (1,73) and (3,12):

    Slope a1 = (y3 − y1)/(x3 − x1)  mod p
       = (12 − 73)/(3 − 1) = (−61)/2 mod 101
  −61 mod 101 = 40
  2⁻¹ mod 101 = 51   (since 2·51 = 102 ≡ 1)
  a1 = 40 · 51 mod 101 = 2040 mod 101 = 20  ✓

Secret S = y1 − a1·x1 = 73 − 20·1 = 53
(or S = y3 − a1·x3 = 12 − 60 = −48 mod 101 = 53)

    Any other pair gives the same line and the same intercept.

    Python check (modular inverse) 
    p = 101
shares = [(1,73),(3,12)]
# Lagrange at x=0: S = Σ yj · ∏ (−xm)/(xj−xm)  mod p
def modinv(a, p): return pow(a, -1, p)  # Python 3.8+
x1,y1 = shares[0]; x2,y2 = shares[1]
c1 = (-x2) * modinv(x1 - x2, p) % p  # = 3·inv(−2) but mod 101
c2 = (-x1) * modinv(x2 - x1, p) % p
# concretely: c1 = 3/2 mod 101 = 52, c2 = 1/(−2) = 50
S = (y1*c1 + y2*c2) % p  # 53
print(c1, c2, S)
    A thief steals only share (2, 93). They guess the secret is 53 — how would you prove they are no better than guessing blind?
    Fix the stolen point (2,93). For any candidate secret S' in 0…100, there is exactly one line through (0,S') and (2,93) — its slope is (93−S')·inv(2) mod 101. Since a₁ was uniform random, every S' is realized by exactly one equally likely slope. The single point rules out nothing — 101 possibilities remain equally likely.
    A line through one dot can tilt to hit any height at the flagpole — so one dot tells you zero about that height.
    🛑 Pause & reflect

    Before the parabola: if the line case uses modulus 101, why can't we just use ordinary integers and skip the mod? What would leak?

    Expert answerWithout a wrap-around, heights grow with the secret. Share (1, 1073) would scream "the secret is large" because f(1)=S+a₁ with integer a₁. Over a prime field, everything wraps uniformly — a share of 93 is no more likely from a large or small secret. The field is what gives the uniform-random guarantee.

    A 3-of-5 sharing with a parabola (degree 2)

    State

    For t = 3, hide S as the constant term of a random parabola f(x)=S+a₁x+a₂x² mod p. Now any 3 points pin the parabola; any 2 leave infinitely many parabolas through them with every possible intercept.

    Generation is the same: pick a₁,a₂ random, evaluate at x=1…n.

    Illustrate

    Keep numbers tiny so you can follow every step. Choose prime p=17, secret S=10, random a₁=6, a₂=3:

    f(x) = 10 + 6x + 3x²  mod 17

f(1)=10+6+3=19 mod17=2   → (1,2)
f(2)=10+12+12=34 mod17=0  → (2,0)
f(3)=10+18+27=55 mod17=4  → (3,4)
f(4)=10+24+48=82 mod17=14 → (4,14)
f(5)=10+30+75=115 mod17=13→ (5,13)

    Rebuild from any 3. Use (1,2),(2,0),(4,14). Lagrange at x=0 says:

    S = Σ yj · Lj(0)  mod p
Lj(0) = ∏_{m≠j} (−xm)/(xj − xm)

L1(0)= (−2·−4)/((1−2)(1−4))=8/3   mod17 → 8·inv3=8·6=48 mod17=14
L2(0)= (−1·−4)/((2−1)(2−4))=4/−2  → 4·inv15=4·8=32 mod17=15
L3(0)= (−1·−2)/((4−1)(4−2))=2/6   → 2·inv6=2·3=6  mod17=6

S =2·14 +0·15 +14·6=28+0+84=112 mod17=10 ✓

    Take a different triple, say (3,4),(4,14),(5,13) — same S=10. Any pair, though, leaves the intercept completely open: through (1,2),(2,0) there is one parabola for each possible S' (choose a₂ to satisfy it).

    An attacker holds two shares (1,2) and (3,4) from the parabola scheme. They think there are only 17 possibilities left — are they right, and does that help?
    They are right about the count but wrong that it helps. There are exactly 17 parabolas through those two points — one for each possible secret 0…16 — each equally likely because (a₁,a₂) were uniform random. So the two shares induce a uniform distribution over all 17 secrets. Zero bits of information about which one is real. That is the same guarantee as Chapter 1, now for t=3.
    A parabola through two dots can still bow up or down to hit any flagpole height — you need the third dot to lock the bow.
    🔴 Deep dive The Lagrange formula and why division needs a prime

    Given t points (xj, yj), the unique degree < t polynomial through them is:

    f(x)= Σ yj · ∏_{m≠j} (x − xm)/(xj − xm)

    We only need f(0), so at x=0 it simplifies to the line above with (−xm)/(xj−xm). Division means "multiply by the modular inverse." The inverse of a mod prime p is the number a⁻¹ with a·a⁻¹ ≡ 1 mod p, found via the extended Euclidean algorithm — or pow(a, -1, p) in Python 3.8+.

    def shamir_reconstruct(shares, p):
    # shares: list of (x,y), len = t
    S = 0
    for j,(xj,yj) in enumerate(shares):
        num, den = 1, 1
        for m,(xm,_) in enumerate(shares):
            if m==j: continue
            num = num * (-xm) % p
            den = den * (xj - xm) % p
        Lj = num * pow(den, -1, p) % p
        S = (S + yj * Lj) % p
    return S

    Critical rule: do everything mod p, never with real-number fractions. Fractions would leak the size of the secret and fail on wrap-around. The prime guarantees every non-zero denominator has an inverse; with a composite modulus some divisions would be impossible.

    In the wild

    Shamir's one-page construction left theory quickly. Any system that says "n operators, any t can recover" is usually running this exact arithmetic.

    The most visible example is HashiCorp Vault's seal / unseal. By default Vault splits its master encryption key (the "root key") with Shamir. A typical production init is vault operator init -key-shares=5 -key-threshold=3: five unseal keys are printed, any three reconstruct the root key to decrypt the storage backend. No single operator ever holds the root key, and the Vault process itself discards it after sealing — the Hill-and-Stakes idea in production since 2015.

    The same pattern appears wherever a single key is too sensitive to store: offline certificate-authority keys, cryptocurrency custody (threshold wallets derive from Shamir plus extra hardening in Chapter 4), and the DNSSEC root key ceremony — where trusted community representatives contribute shares that together sign the internet's root zone.

    Why p must exceed n and the secret. We need distinct non-zero x values 1…n, all different mod p, and the secret must be a field element. Hence p > max(secret, n). Real deployments use p = 2⁸, 2²⁵⁶, or a 256-bit prime — far above any n of 5 or 10. Shamir's paper assumed data D is "a number" and works inside a finite field with |F| = |S|.

    Active recall — Question
    Alice shares S among 5 with threshold 3 using p=17. You steal 2 shares. How many possible secrets remain, and are they equally likely?
    Answer
    All 17 possible secrets remain, equally likely — exactly one parabola per candidate secret passes through your 2 points, each from an equally likely coefficient draw. Zero leakage.

    Where it breaks

    Shamir solves the flexibility half. The remaining cracks are the ones every real deployment learns about.

    ⚠️ Common misconception"Shamir works over ordinary integers." People implement f(x) = S + a₁x + … without a modulus and wonder why one share reveals that S is small. Integer evaluations leak size. The wrap-around of a finite field is not an optimization — it is the security.
    🚫 Edge caseSmall fields enumerate. Demo prime 17 is pedagogical. An attacker who steals t−1 shares still faces only 17 guesses — brute-forceable offline. Production primes are ≥ 2¹²⁸. Second edge: x = 0 is forbidden as a share — that point is the secret itself. Third: the dealer sees everything. Whoever picks the polynomial learns S and all shares. Real ceremonies use distributed coin-tossing (Chapter 4).

    How this connects

    Chapter 1 gave perfect hiding but no flexibility. Chapter 2 gives both. What remains is proving the guarantee and hardening the construction:

    • Chapter 3 — Why It's Unbreakable makes the "equally likely" argument rigorous with finite-field counting and contrasts it with computational assumptions (RSA, discrete log).
    • Chapter 4 — Hardening adds cheater detection: how to catch a courier who lies about their stake height (verifiable secret sharing) and how to refresh shares without changing the secret.
    • Chapters 5–7 — From Shares to Computation keep Shamir as the inner layer and add interaction, so parties can compute on shared secrets without ever rebuilding them.

    The whole picture

    The hook asked for slips where any 2 of 3 open the vault but one slip is useless. A line does it: hide 53 as the intercept, pick a random slope, hand out heights at x = 1, 2, 3. One height leaves every intercept possible; two heights pin the line and reveal 53.

    Generalize the degree and you generalize the threshold. A parabola makes 3-of-5, a cubic makes 4-of-7 — each extra degree trades one more random coefficient for one more point needed to pin the curve. Shares stay the size of the secret, and t−1 shares are statistical noise.

    The bank now has a true quorum system. What it does not yet have is a way to catch lies, rotate shares, or compute without rebuilding — and it still trusts the dealer who drew the hill.

    If you remember only one thing: The secret is the y-intercept of a random polynomial; Shares are points on it — t points rebuild it, t−1 leave it uniformly random.
    🚀 Launch pad · one step beyond

    The integer-division trap

    Bob re-implements Shamir's 2-of-3 (p=101, S=53, a₁=20) but "simplifies" reconstruction with real-number division: he computes c₁ = 3/2 = 1.5 and c₂ = 1/(−2) = −0.5, then S = 73·1.5 + 12·(−0.5) = 103.5. He rounds to 103 and fails. Worse, when he does brute-force over integers without mod, single-share values leak whether S is large. Explain both failures in one argument: why must Lagrange coefficients be computed as modular inverses, not fractions, and why the field matters for both correctness and secrecy? Generalize to S=1234, p=2087, shares (1,1494),(2,1942) from the literature — show the modular reconstruction that gives 1234.

    Stuck? Reveal a hint at a time.
    Hint 1 — reframeOver integers, 1/2 is not an integer. Over mod 101, "1/2" means "the number ·2 ≡ 1" which is 51. Fractions discard modular wrap-around. Test: 2·51 mod 101 = 102 mod 101 = 1. Real 0.5 ·2 = 1 but ignores mod.
    Hint 2 — key conceptRecompute with mod inverses: for p=101, inv(2)=51, c₁=3·51=153 mod101=52, c₂=−1·51 mod101=50, S=73·52+12·50=53 mod101. For p=2087, inv(1−2)=inv(−1)=2086, c₁=2·2086=4172 mod2087=... work it with (−xm)/(xj−xm).
    Hint 3 — setupFor S=1234 example: p=2087, shares (1,1494),(2,1942),(3,491) with poly 1234+166x+94x². Pick any 3, compute Lj(0) mod 2087 using extended Euclid, verify sum =1234. Single share without mod would leak monotonic growth; with mod every share is uniform 0…p−1 regardless of S.

    Glossary

    Polynomial f(x) = S + a₁x + … + a_{t−1}x^{t−1}
    The random curve whose intercept is the secret. In the Hill Model: the hidden hill shape through the flagpole height.
    Share (i, f(i))
    One point on the curve at a public x. In the Hill Model: height measured at stake i.
    Threshold t
    Minimum shares that can rebuild; degree is t−1. In the Hill Model: stakes needed to pin the hill.
    Lagrange interpolation
    Formula that rebuilds the unique polynomial through t points and reads f(0) without finding all coefficients. In the Hill Model: deducing the flagpole height from stake heights without mapping the whole hill.
    Modular inverse (a⁻¹ mod p)
    Number with a·a⁻¹ ≡ 1 mod p; needed for "division" in the field. In the Hill Model: the wrap-around arithmetic that keeps heights uniform.
    Finite field GF(p) (p prime)
    The clock arithmetic system where every non-zero element has an inverse — what makes the uniform-random guarantee hold. In the Hill Model: the periodic terrain that hides whether the flagpole is tall or short.
    Ideal secret sharing
    Each share is as large as the secret (no blow-up). Shamir and XOR sharing are ideal; Blakley is not. In the Hill Model: each stake sheet is one height number, same size as the flagpole height.

    Knowledge check

    3 questions. Pick the best answer for each and click Check answers.

    1. A dealer shares S with t=3, p large. You obtain 2 shares (x₁,y₁),(x₂,y₂). What can you say about S?
    Why: Correct is uniform over all p possibilities. Two points under-constrain a parabola (needs three) — for each candidate intercept there is exactly one parabola through the three points, all from equally likely random coefficients.
    2. Which reconstruction uses the correct finite-field step for shares (1,73),(3,12), p=101?
    Why: Correct uses Lagrange coefficients (−xm)/(xj−xm) with modular inverses. Real fractions or averages discard the field wrap-around and give wrong intercepts. Option D is the additive-sharing rule — wrong scheme.
    3. Why is storing the polynomial coefficients, rather than points, insecure for threshold sharing?
    Why: Correct is the (n,n) collapse. If you split coefficients, you need all of them to get S = a₀ — and a single holder of a₁ trivially narrows the line's possible intercepts. Points keep coefficients hidden and threshold flexible.
