    Chapter 4: Hardening the Scheme

    TL;DR. Publish commitments to the hill so anyone can spot a lying stake, and re-hill without moving the flagpole — then remove the dealer entirely.
    🎣 The hook Alice's bank has a perfect 2-of-3 sharing: any two stakes rebuild 53, one teaches nothing. Then courier 2 submits a fake height of 99 and the quorum rebuilds the wrong secret. And the dealer who created the hill — Alice herself — already knows every share. The math was perfect. The trust was not.

    Prerequisites

    • Chapters 1–3: Shamir sharing, Lagrange reconstruction, information-theoretic proof, prime fields
    • Comfort with "hash/commitment" intuition — a fingerprint that binds but does not reveal

    By the end you will be able to…

    • Explain why plain Shamir fails against a cheating share-holder or a cheating dealer
    • Verify Feldman commitments by hand on a tiny example and spot a bad share before reconstruction
    • Describe proactive refresh (same secret, new random hill) and dealer-free DKG at a high level
    • Weigh when additive+XOR is enough vs when you need VSS/Pedersen hardness

    The foundational truth

    Shamir's security proof assumes two honesties: the dealer picks the hill fairly, and participants submit true stake heights at rebuild time.

    Remove either and the scheme fails silently. A cheating dealer can give inconsistent shares that different quorums reconstruct to different secrets. A cheating participant can submit a wrong height and make an honest quorum recover the wrong flagpole height, with no way to tell who lied.

    Real systems live where neither trust holds — servers are compromised over months, insiders collude. Hardening keeps the hill math but adds witnesses who can check it.

    A familiar bridge — receipts for the hill survey

    Keep the Hill-and-Stakes image, but add a notary. Call it the Receipts Model.

    • Commitment → a public receipt taped to the courthouse wall for each coefficient of the hill (e.g., "commitment to slope" ). The receipt hides the number but fingerprints it — change the slope and the fingerprint mismatches.
    • Share verification → each courier checks their stake height against the receipts before accepting it. No private channel needed — the receipts are public.
    • Refresh → periodically the couriers jointly pick a new hill with **zero** at the flagpole (a hill of height 0) and add it to their shares — flagpole unchanged, stakes all new.

    The receipts turn "trust the surveyor and trust each other" into "trust the math on the wall."

    ⚠️ Before we build further — most people leave this bridge thinking…

    "Adding a hash of the secret to the shares lets you verify — just check the reconstructed secret's hash"

    This is wrong because a single bad share still forces you to reconstruct before you can check — and you won't know *which* share lied.

    What's actually true: You need a commitment that lets each share be verified *individually*, before any quorum combines them — that's what Feldman/Pedersen receipts give.

    Building the model, one piece at a time

    Three hardening moves, cheapest first.

    Verifiable Secret Sharing — catching a lying stake

    State

    Verifiable Secret Sharing (VSS) publishes a commitment to each polynomial coefficient so holders can test their share without seeing others. The simplest is Feldman VSS (1987): pick a cyclic group where discrete log is hard, publish Cj = g^{aj} mod p' for each coefficient aj (including a0 = S). Holder i checks:

    g^{f(i)} ≟ C0 · C1^{i} · C2^{i²} … · C_{t−1}^{i^{t−1}}   (in the group)

    If a dealer gave a bad share, the equality fails and the holder raises an alarm before reconstruction. Pedersen VSS strengthens this by hiding C0 as well (computational hiding even for the secret), at the cost of an extra random polynomial.

    Illustrate

    Tiny demo over group mod 7 with generator g=3 (not production size — just to see the arithmetic). Shamir from Chapter 2 line: S=53→53 mod7=4, but we use small numbers: let p=7, polynomial f(x)=4+2x mod7 (S=4, slope 2). Feldman publishes:

    C0 = g^4 = 3^4=81 mod7=4
C1 = g^2 = 3^2=9 mod7=2
Share for i=1: f(1)=6 → check: g^6=3^6=729 mod7=1
  RHS: C0·C1^1 =4·2=8 mod7=1  ✓ matches

Share for i=2: f(2)=1 (4+4=8 mod7=1) → g^1=3
  RHS: C0·C1^2 =4·2^2=4·4=16 mod7=2  … mismatch?

We cheated by mixing two moduli. In reality Feldman uses
a share modulus p and a group order q dividing p−1, with
q > secret space — the separation matters. Pedagogically:
the *structure* is "commit to coefficients, recombine with
same linear combo as Lagrange," and the equality is checked
in the commitment group, not the share field.

    The point survives the toy mismatch: each holder recomputes the RHS from public Cj and their i, then compares to g^{share}. A dealer who gave (1, 99) instead of (1,6) would fail the check — g^99 ≠ C0·C1 — so the bad share is rejected immediately, without a quorum.

    # Pseudocode (Feldman verify)
def verify_share(i, yi, commitments, g, q):
    # commitments[j] = g^{aj} mod p', q is group order
    lhs = pow(g, yi, p_prime)
    rhs = 1
    for j, Cj in enumerate(commitments):
        rhs = rhs * pow(Cj, pow(i, j, q), p_prime) % p_prime
    return lhs == rhs
    A dealer publishes correct commitments but sends Alice the wrong share. Alice's verify fails. Could Alice have faked the failure to frame an honest dealer?
    No — the commitments are public and the check is deterministic. Anyone can recompute the expected RHS for i=1 from the public Cj and see it equals g^6, not g^{whatever Alice claims}. If Alice truly received a bad share she can broadcast it; others see it fails the same check. The receipt is judged by math, not by who complains.
    Post fingerprints of the hill's coefficients on the courthouse wall — then each stake height can be checked against those fingerprints alone.
    🛑 Pause & reflect

    Why can't we just sign each share with the dealer's private key instead of using Feldman commitments?

    Expert answerA signature proves the dealer sent that share, but not that all shares lie on one consistent polynomial. A malicious dealer could sign inconsistent shares — signatures would all verify, yet different quorums would reconstruct different secrets and no one could pin blame. Commitments bind the *whole* polynomial, so inconsistency is detectable from the public receipts alone.

    Dealer-free and fresh — DKG and proactive refresh

    State

    Two remaining trusts to remove: (1) the dealer who knows all shares at creation time, and (2) the slow leakage where an attacker who steals one share per year eventually collects t.

    Distributed Key Generation (DKG) removes (1): each participant deals its own random polynomial and acts as dealer for a sub-share; the combined secret is the sum of constant terms, and no one ever knows the full set. Proactive Secret Sharing (PSS) removes (2): at intervals, parties jointly generate a random polynomial with constant term 0, add its evaluations to their existing shares, and erase the old values. The flagpole stays at S, but every stake is new — old stolen shares become worthless.

    Illustrate

    Proactive refresh, line case t=2: starting shares from f(x)=53+20x mod101 are (1,73),(2,93),(3,12). Parties jointly pick random zero-hill z(x)=0+14x (constant 0, random slope 14):

    z(1)=14, z(2)=28, z(3)=42
New shares = old + z(i) mod101:
  (1, 73+14=87), (2,93+28=20) [121 mod101=20], (3,12+42=54)
Any 2 still rebuild 53 (because z(0)=0): check (1,87),(2,20):
  slope = (20−87)/1 = −67 mod101=34 → 14+20=34 ✓, S=87−34=53
Old stolen share (1,73) no longer sits on the new line.

    DKG at a glance (3 parties, t=2): each picks random si and line fi(x)=si+ai·x, deals sub-shares to others with VSS. Final share for party i is sum of received sub-shares; final secret is s1+s2+s3. No dealer ever assembled it.

    Party1 deals f1, Party2 deals f2, Party3 deals f3
Final S = f1(0)+f2(0)+f3(0)   — never assembled centrally
Final share(i)= f1(i)+f2(i)+f3(i)  — each Pi sums what it received
    Could an attacker who stole share (1,73) before the refresh combine it with stolen (2,20) after the refresh to rebuild?
    No — they lie on different lines. (1,73) was on the old hill; (2,20) is on the new hill (old plus z). Mixing eras reconstructs to a random value, not S. Proactive refresh achieves forward security against mobile adversaries as long as fewer than t shares are stolen within a single epoch.
    Refresh the hill by adding a new zero-height hill — stakes all move, flagpole does not.
    🔴 Deep dive Feldman vs Pedersen — hiding the secret in the commitment

    Feldman publishes g^S directly — an observer learns something about S if S has low entropy (dictionary attack on small secrets). Pedersen fixes this by using two generators g, h and a blinding polynomial: commitment to S is g^S · h^{S'} where S' is random. Then even C0 is statistically hiding, and the verification equation gains an extra factor. The cost is one extra field element per share. In practice, Vault and many DKGs use Feldman when S is a high-entropy key and Pedersen when human passwords are shared.

    # Pedersen share: (f(i), f'(i)) where f'(x) is blinding poly
# Commitment: Cj = g^{aj} · h^{bj}
# Verify: g^{f(i)}·h^{f'(i)} ≟ ∏ Cj^{i^j}

    In the wild

    Three places you meet these receipts in practice.

    1. Vault and key ceremonies. HashiCorp Vault's Shamir seal in its simplest form trusts the initializer as dealer. Operators who cannot tolerate that run auto-unseal via HSM/KMS — or layer a DKG so no single initializer ever knows the combined key. Rekey (vault operator rekey) is a manual proactive refresh: new random polynomials, same barrier key re-wrapped, old shares invalidated.

    2. Threshold wallets and custody. When a crypto custody quorum is 3-of-5 executives, Feldman/Pedersen VSS lets any executive verify their key shard against on-chain commitments before signing a transaction. Without it, a malicious dealer could give one executive a bad shard and frame them when signing fails.

    3. DKG in distributed validators. Protocols like Ethereum distributed validator DKG (e.g., Obol, SSV) run a Pedersen DKG among operators to generate a validator key where no operator ever knows the whole private key — shares are assembled additively as described above, with VSS transcripts publicly verifiable on-chain.

    Active recall — Question
    Which failure does Feldman VSS catch, and which does it not catch alone?
    Answer
    Catches: bad share from dealer or cheater at rebuild — each share verified vs public commitments before use. Does not catch: dealer knowledge (dealer still knows all shares — need DKG) or slow share-stealing over time (need proactive refresh).

    Where it breaks

    Commitments change the security type. Plain Shamir was information-theoretic (Chapter 3). Hardened schemes trade that for computational hardness — discrete log must stay hard.

    ⚠️ Common misconception"VSS makes Shamir information-theoretic again." Feldman commitments leak g^S — with small secrets, brute force over S and check against C0 recovers S. Pedersen hides computationally instead. Only plain Shamir with no public commitments retains pure information-theoretic hiding of S (and then you lose verifiability).
    🚫 Edge caseTwo silent failure modes: (1) Not erasing old shares after proactive refresh — the window stays open and an attacker who collected t shares across epochs can still win if any epoch had t in one window. Erase matters. (2) Reusing the same Feldman randomness for two secrets — commitments become linkable. Third edge: DKG needs a broadcast channel and dispute phase — without it, a dealer who accuses others of bad shares can stall the ceremony.

    The whole picture

    The hook's two cheats — a lying courier with height 99 and a dealer who already knew 53 — are the cracks plain Shamir leaves open. Receipts close the first: publish g^{aj} on the courthouse wall and every stake can be tested alone, before any quorum assembles.

    Zero-hills close slow leakage: add a random 0-at-flagpole hill and every stake is fresh while the flagpole stands. Distributed dealing closes the last trust: no one ever draws the whole hill alone.

    The price is the shift from envelope to safe: commitments rest on discrete log — computationally hard, not information-theoretically perfect. That trade is why most production systems publish Feldman receipts for high-entropy keys and Pedersen blinding for human secrets.

    If you remember only one thing: Publish fingerprints of the polynomial so bad shares fail alone — then add zero-hills to age out stolen shares.
    🚀 Launch pad · one step beyond

    Design a mobile adversary

    An attacker can steal one share per month from a (3,5) deployment. Without proactive refresh, they win in 3 months. With monthly refresh and perfect erasure, they never win unless they steal 3 in one month. Model a weaker erasure: party P3 fails to delete old shares. How many shares can the attacker collect over 4 months by targeting P3 plus one new victim each month? Which epoch-mixing composition still lets them rebuild? Propose a detection rule the honest parties could run before reconstruction that would catch the stale share, and name which VSS property it uses.

    Stuck? Reveal a hint at a time.
    Hint 1 — reframeWithout erasure, stolen sets stack across epochs. P3 holds shares from epochs 0,1,2,3 at stake 3 — four different hills at the same x but different flagpole-preserving offsets.
    Hint 2 — key conceptAttacker steals (3@epoch0), then (1@epoch1), then (2@epoch2) — but (3@epoch0)+(1@epoch1)+(2@epoch2) lie on no single hill, so naive mix yields garbage. To win they need 3 shares from one epoch — target P3's retained old share plus two fresh from the same month.
    Hint 3 — setupDetection: verify each presented share against the *current* epoch's commitments Cj (which change after refresh). Stale shares fail Feldman check for the new hill — exactly the per-share verifiability from this chapter.

    Glossary

    Verifiable Secret Sharing (VSS)
    Shamir plus public commitments so each share is individually testable. In the Receipts Model: fingerprints of each hill coefficient on the wall.
    Feldman VSS
    VSS where Cj=g^{aj}; verifies but leaks g^S for low-entropy S. Computational security (discrete log). Receipts that hide the number but fingerprint it.
    Pedersen VSS
    VSS with blinding — Cj=g^{aj}·h^{bj} — hiding even g^S statistically. Receipts with an extra random layer.
    Distributed Key Generation (DKG)
    N dealers each share a random si; final secret is sum si, no one knew it alone. Hill drawn by committee, no single surveyor.
    Proactive Secret Sharing (PSS)
    Periodic refresh adding a random zero-secret polynomial; old shares expire. New zero-height hill added, flagpole fixed.
    Mobile adversary
    Attacker who steals few shares per epoch, hoping to collect t over time. Thief who visits one courier per month.

    Knowledge check

    3 questions. Pick the best answer for each and click Check answers.

    1. Why does checking g^{f(i)} vs ∏ Cj^{i^j} catch a lying share before a quorum forms?
    Why: Correct is per-share verification. The RHS is computed from public Cj and public i; LHS is g^{claimed share}. No quorum needed — a bad share fails alone, so you know who lied without reconstructing.
    2. Which statement about proactive refresh is true?
    Why: Correct is the zero-hill addition. The secret (flagpole) is unchanged; the hill (polynomial) is re-randomized, so cross-epoch mixes lie on no single hill.
    3. Feldman vs Pedersen trade-off?
    Why: Correct captures the blinding cost. Feldman publishes g^S directly (fine for 256-bit keys, not passwords); Pedersen adds hiding with g^S·h^{S'}.
