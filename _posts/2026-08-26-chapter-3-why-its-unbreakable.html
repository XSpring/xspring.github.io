    Chapter 3: Why It's Unbreakable

    TL;DR. Mod-prime wrap-around makes t−1 shares match every possible secret exactly once — uniform and unsqueezable, even with infinite compute.
    🎣 The hook Chapter 2 showed you *how* a line hides 53. Now the suspicious question: could a supercomputer stare at one share (1, 73) mod 101 and say "53 is a bit more likely than 12"? If yes, the whole scheme is decoration. If no, what math guarantees it?

    Prerequisites

    • Chapters 1–2: additive vs Shamir sharing, the line example S=53, p=101, f(x)=53+20x
    • Comfort with "equally likely" reasoning — counting possibilities, not formulas
    • Willingness to think about "all computers in the world" as still not enough

    By the end you will be able to…

    • Explain why arithmetic modulo a prime gives each non-zero number an inverse
    • Run the counting proof that t−1 Shamir shares leave every secret equally likely
    • Contrast information-theoretic security with computational security (RSA, AES)
    • Identify why composite moduli, small fields, and integer arithmetic break the guarantee

    The foundational truth

    The guarantee we need is stronger than "hard to guess." It is: even knowing t−1 shares, every possible secret remains exactly as likely as before you saw them.

    Mathematicians call this information-theoretic security (security against any amount of computation, even infinite). It is different from the "no one has found a fast way yet" security that protects RSA or AES.

    You already use the distinction. A sealed envelope hides the letter perfectly until opened — no amount of staring guesses it. A locked safe hides the combination only until someone tries every number. Shamir aims for the envelope, not the safe.

    The tool that creates the envelope is the finite field (clock arithmetic where you can add, multiply, and *divide* and stay inside the clock). Without division, shares would cluster and leak size — with it, they spread uniformly.

    A familiar bridge — the clock dial

    Use one image for the whole chapter. Call it the Clock Dial Model.

    Take a clock with a prime number of hours — say 101 hours numbered 0…100. At midnight (0) is the flagpole height from Chapter 2. Every arithmetic step wraps around the dial. On a 101-hour clock, 100+5 = 4 (wrap), and crucially, for any non-zero hour h, there is exactly one hour h⁻¹ with h·h⁻¹ = 1 (the inverse hour).

    • Prime modulus p → hours on the dial (p must be prime to give every hour an inverse)
    • Share → a position on the dial read at a stake number
    • t−1 shares → knowing a few positions still leaves midnight equally likely to be any hour

    The clock is why Chapter 2 insisted on mod p. Without the clock, numbers grow and betray whether the secret was large.

    ⚠️ Before we build further — most people leave this bridge thinking…

    "If t−1 shares look random, that's good enough — 'close to random' is the same as secure"

    This is wrong because "close to random" still leaks bias an attacker can amplify over many attempts — perfect security requires exact uniformity, not approximation.

    What's actually true: Shamir guarantees exact uniformity: for any t−1 shares and any candidate secret, there are equally many random polynomials consistent with that guess — precisely p^{t-1−(t−1)} = 1 per guess, uniformly.

    Building the model, one piece at a time

    We build the field, then the counting proof, then the contrast with computational schemes.

    Finite fields — why primes, not just any modulus

    State

    Finite field GF(p) (Galois field of prime p) is the set {0,…,p−1} with addition and multiplication mod p, where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse (a partner hour that multiplies to 1).

    This inverse is what lets Lagrange interpolation divide by (xj−xm) mod p. If p were composite (say 6), element 2 would have no inverse mod 6 — you could not divide by 2, and some share sets would be unsolvable or leak.

    Illustrate

    List inverses mod 7 (prime): 1⁻¹=1, 2⁻¹=4 (2·4=8≡1), 3⁻¹=5, 4⁻¹=2, 5⁻¹=3, 6⁻¹=6 — every non-zero hour finds its partner. Mod 6 (composite): 2·3=0 mod6 — zero divisors appear, so 2 has no inverse. Try solving slope (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁) mod 6 when x₂−x₁=2 — impossible.

    p=7 prime:  extended Euclid gives 3⁻¹=5 because 3·5=15≡1
p=6 composite: gcd(2,6)=2 ≠1  →  no inverse, division fails

    Production choice: for byte strings, we use GF(2⁸) — polynomials over bits with a different wrap-around — so every 8-bit chunk has an inverse. For number secrets, a large prime like p = 257 or p > 2²⁵⁶ works.

    If addition and multiplication mod p are just "wrap-around," why isn't p=100 fine? 100 has wrap-around too.
    Wrap-around alone gives a ring, not a field. With p=100, factor sharing breaks: 2 and 50 multiply to 0 mod 100, so neither has an inverse — you cannot divide by 2 with certainty. Lagrange needs to divide by every (xj−xm). Non-zero differences must be invertible, so p must be prime (or a prime-power field like GF(2⁸)).
    A prime clock is special: every non-zero hour can undo its own multiplication — that's what lets the hill math divide cleanly.
    🛑 Pause & reflect

    If p must be prime for division, why can't we just use ordinary fractions (real numbers) and skip primes entirely?

    Expert answerReal numbers have inverses, but they betray size. A share y = S + a₁·x with integer a₁ grows with S — the share value leaks whether S was large. Mod-prime wraps everything uniformly to 0…p−1, so a share of 73 is equally likely from S=53 or S=12 with different random slopes. The field gives uniformity, not just solvability.

    The counting proof — one polynomial per guess

    State

    Proof sketch: Fix any t−1 shares at distinct x₁…x_{t−1} with values y₁…y_{t−1}, and any candidate secret S' (value at x=0). There is exactly one polynomial of degree < t through the t points (0,S'),(x₁,y₁),…,(x_{t−1},y_{t−1}). So the adversary's view is consistent with every S' equally.

    Why equally likely? The dealer chose the t−1 non-constant coefficients a₁…a_{t−1} uniformly at random. Each S' corresponds bijectively to one coefficient vector that would produce the observed shares. All p^{t−1} coefficient vectors were equally likely, so all p candidate secrets inherit equal probability.

    Illustrate

    Concrete with Chapter 2's line (t=2). Adversary sees (1,73), p=101. For each S' ∈ 0…100:

    Line through (0,S') and (1,73): slope = 73 − S' mod 101
S'=53 → slope 20  (our real line 53+20x)
S'=12 → slope 61
S'=0  → slope 73
… 100 distinct slopes, one per S'

    Only one slope equals the dealer's random draw. Since the slope was uniform, the posterior probability of each S' after seeing (1,73) stays 1/101 — exactly the prior. No update, no leakage.

    Parabola case (t=3) with shares (1,2),(2,0) from Chapter 2, p=17: for each S' there is exactly one parabola through (0,S'),(1,2),(2,0). Three points pin one parabola; the remaining free coefficient counts cancel. So 17 candidates stay uniform with probability 1/17.

    # Count: p^(t−1) total polynomials = 17²=289
# Consistent with 2 shares + a fixed S': p^(t−1−2)=17⁰=1 per S'
# Uniform across p=17 values of S'
    Does "uniform for every guess" mean that two candidate secrets could still have different numbers of matching coefficient vectors if p were composite?
    Yes — and that is the failure mode. With composite modulus, not every denominator is invertible, so some S' have zero matching polynomials while others have many — probabilities become non-uniform and shares leak. Primality makes the count exactly one per S', guaranteeing perfect balance.
    One stolen stake leaves the flagpole height perfectly hidden — every possible height needs exactly one hill that fits the stake, each equally likely.

    Information-theoretic vs. computational security

    State

    Information-theoretic (Shamir, XOR additive) means security holds even if the attacker is unbounded — tries every guess with infinite time. The proof is counting, not hardness. Computational security (RSA, discrete log, AES) means security holds only if the attacker is limited to feasible computation — "no one knows a fast algorithm yet."

    Shamir belongs to the first camp because t−1 shares contain literally zero Shannon information about S (entropy unchanged). AES with a 128-bit key has at most 128 bits of uncertainty, but in principle brute force would reveal it given enough universes of time.

    Illustrate

    Analogy: envelope vs. combination lock. The envelope (Shamir) hides the message information-theoretically — no amount of squeezing the paper reveals the ink inside. The lock (RSA) hides it computationally — the combination is well-hidden but still a finite search.

    Concrete numbers: IBM's 2024 breach data showed credential-based breaches taking ~292 days to contain. If a secret were additively or Shamir-shared with t=3, stealing 2 shares would give the attacker still 0 bits of advantage — contain time becomes irrelevant for that secret. Replace it with an AES key on one server and the same theft gives the whole key.

    If information-theoretic is strictly stronger, why does anyone use RSA/AES instead of just secret-sharing everything?
    Because perfect sharing has costs: shares must be as large as the secret (ideal bound), you need interaction to use the secret without rebuilding it, and someone must store multiple shares. AES/RSA give compact, locally usable keys under a computational assumption we tolerate. PETs compose both — share the AES key with Shamir, then compute over shares.
    🔴 Deep dive Entropy view — "zero information" made precise

    Let S be uniform over GF(p) (entropy H(S)=log₂ p bits). Let V be the adversary's view of t−1 shares. Shamir's counting shows the joint distribution satisfies H(S|V)=H(S) — conditional entropy equals prior entropy. Mutual information I(S;V)=H(S)−H(S|V)=0. That zero is exact, not "small." The one-line intuition: the mapping from random coefficients to share values, conditioned on fixing t−1 shares, is a bijection to field elements, so uniformity of coefficients transfers to uniformity over S.

    # Formal: dealer draws a₁…a_{t−1} uniform in GF(p)
# f(x)=S+a₁x+… mod p, shares yᵢ=f(xᵢ)
# For fixed V, the map S' ↦ (a₁,…,a_{t−1}) is bijective → uniform

    In the wild — where each guarantee matters

    Choose the guarantee that matches the threat. The 1979 papers framed it as robustness: use (k,n) so the system "can function securely even when misfortunes destroy half the pieces and breaches expose all but one of the remaining pieces" Shamir 1979.

    When the adversary is a single compromised server with months of dwell time — the 292-day credential-breach lifecycle in IBM 2024 — computational keys on that server fall. Information-theoretic shares with t=3 and distribution across three independent stores do not: any two stolen shares still leak zero bits, so dwell time does not compound into advantage. That is why HashiCorp Vault's default Shamir seal is 5 shares threshold 3 Vault Seal: the cluster can lose two shares and still unseal, and an attacker with two shares still needs a third independent compromise.

    Conversely, when the bottleneck is performance or reuse (encrypting gigabytes, signing millions of messages), computational primitives win — you Shamir-share the root key once, then derive AES session keys computationally for speed.

    Active recall — Question
    You see 1 share of a (3,5) Shamir sharing over p=101. Do you know more about the secret than someone who saw nothing?
    Answer
    No. Both have 6.66 bits of uncertainty (101 equally likely values). Your share is uniform noise conditioned on nothing — same distribution as ignorance.

    Where it breaks

    The proof assumes its conditions. Break them and the uniform count collapses.

    ⚠️ Common misconception"Bigger p always means more secure." People raise p to 2²⁵⁶ and think threshold security improves. For information-theoretic hiding, security is already perfect for any p > secret — bigger p only enlarges the secret space and brute-force of the whole field (which was already infeasible to enumerate). The gain from 101 to 257 is not "more uniformity" but resistance to guessing the entire secret when its domain is itself huge.
    🚫 Edge caseThree fatal shortcuts: (1) Using integers without a modulus — shares leak magnitude. (2) Using composite p (e.g., 100) — some S' have zero matching polynomials, breaking uniformity. (3) Allowing share x=0 — that is the secret. Also: reusing the same random coefficients for two different secrets — the relation between shares leaks the relation between secrets.
    🔮 PredictBefore Chapter 4: the dealer who generated the hill knows every share. If that dealer is bribed after distribution, the whole proof is still true but useless. What extra mechanism would let couriers verify their stakes without trusting the dealer?

    The whole picture

    The hook asked whether a supercomputer could squeeze a hint from one share of 73. The counting answer is no: over a prime clock, for each of the 101 possible flagpole heights there is exactly one hill through the stolen stake at that height, each from an equally likely random draw. One stake leaves 101 equally likely secrets — same as guessing blind.

    The clock is doing the work. Division exists for every non-zero hour, so Lagrange coefficients exist, so the bijection between guesses and coefficient vectors is perfect, so entropy is untouched. Without the clock — integers or composite moduli — the bijection breaks and leakage appears.

    This is the strongest guarantee cryptography offers, and the most limited: it hides perfectly, but it needs a trusted dealer, honest shares, and fresh randomness. The next chapter removes those trusts.

    If you remember only one thing: With prime-field arithmetic, t−1 shares match every secret exactly once — so they teach nothing, even to an infinite adversary.
    🚀 Launch pad · one step beyond

    Count the bijection

    Formalize the proof for the line case: Fix prime p=17, t=2, adversary sees (1,2). Enumerate all 17 possible secrets S' ∈ 0…16 and compute slope = 2 − S' mod 17 for each. Show the 17 slopes are all distinct and cover 0…16. Then generalize: for t=3 with shares (1,2),(2,0) (p=17), argue that for each S' there is exactly one pair (a₁,a₂) producing those shares, by solving the linear system. Why does this bijection fail when p=6 (composite) — give an S' with zero solutions?

    Stuck? Reveal a hint at a time.
    Hint 1 — reframeFor t=2, the map S' ↦ slope = y₁ − S' mod p is a permutation of 0…p−1 (subtracting S' simply rotates the clock). So distinct S' give distinct slopes, all equally likely.
    Hint 2 — key conceptFor t=3, fix S', x₁=1,x₂=2 with y₁=2,y₂=0: system [1 1; 2 4]·[a₁;a₂]=[y₁−S'; y₂−S'] mod 17 has determinant 2 ≠0 mod 17 → unique solution per S'. Determinant invertibility needs prime field.
    Hint 3 — setupMod 6, determinant 2 shares factor with modulus (gcd(2,6)=2) — not invertible. Choose S' so RHS parity mismatches: no solution. Hence some secrets have zero preimages, others have two — uniformity fails.

    Glossary

    Finite field GF(p) / Clock Dial
    Numbers 0…p−1 with wrap-around where every non-zero has an inverse (needs p prime). In the Hill Model: the periodic hill terrain.
    Multiplicative inverse (a⁻¹ mod p)
    Partner with a·a⁻¹ ≡ 1 mod p; enables division in Lagrange. In the Dial Model: the undo-hour.
    Information-theoretic security
    Zero information leakage even to unbounded attacker; posterior equals prior. In the Hill Model: any flagpole height still needs exactly one hill through the stolen stakes.
    Computational security
    Security assumes bounded attacker — breaking would take infeasible time (RSA, AES). In the Hill Model: a lock that resists picking, not an envelope that hides.
    Entropy H(S), conditional H(S|V)
    Bits of uncertainty; perfect hiding means H(S|V)=H(S). In the Hill Model: stolen stakes do not shrink the set of plausible flagpole heights.
